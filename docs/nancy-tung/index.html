San Francisco’s Democratic Party is axles up in a ditch.
Chair Nancy Tung’s party supporters say she’s navigated the party into this place with “integrity and vision.” Good to know: Insofar as there’s a way out, it will be Tung who’s behind the wheel.
It has, indeed, been a rough road of late for the county Democratic Party.
The local party reversed its own bylaws so it could in August buck the California Democratic Party and oppose Prop. 40, the “Billionaire Tax.” Last month, the local party voted handily to spurn attempts to mitigate the city’s contract with controversial surveillance camera outfit Flock — not one week before Mayor Daniel Lurie enacted attempts to do just that.
Complicating matters, Tung in August accepted a $10,000 donation from crypto billionaire Chris Larsen; she used some of that money to pay for airfare to an Atlanta confab put on by Flock.
Larsen is a major contributor to the massive-dollar effort to sink Prop. 40 and has put millions of dollars into installing Flock cameras throughout San Francisco. Tung told the San Francisco Standard that the donation had “no bearing whatsoever” on her actions.
Also in August, the San Francisco Democratic Party endorsed Manny Yekutiel for District 8 supervisor, despite an on-the-record allegation of sexual assault by Brad Chapin. This came after a five-week “investigation” into that allegation.
On the very day of the vote, party members were delivered a half-page letter tersely noting that the accusation against Yekutiel did not come under the aegis of the party’s anti-harassment policy and its “investigation” could neither confirm nor deny whether an assault occurred.
As the party policy only covers actions undertaken at party-linked events, and Chapin alleges he was assaulted at a private home, it should have been rapidly determined that the Democratic Party could not take this matter up. Instead, the “investigation” was allowed to drag on for weeks and, in the end, its non-findings were used to buttress the endorsement of the party’s preferred candidate.
And then: Axles up in a ditch.
Following the party’s endorsement of Yekutiel, Mayor Lurie also backed him, adding that he believed Yekutiel’s account that Chapin was not telling the truth. This, it seems, led directly to two more men this month coming forward to accuse Yekutiel of sexual misconduct. It warrants mentioning that Lurie appears to have, counter-intuitively sunk Yekutiel’s chances by endorsing him.
The party will hold an emergency meeting on Oct. 11 to reconsider its District 8 endorsement. It promises to be a fraught affair.
Tung has since apologized for this outcome and this process. But not before her detractors have called for her to do more — and step down. A planned Wednesday Richmond District press conference intended to bolster Tung in the face of this criticism was abruptly canceled, leaving befuddled members of the media to instead interview anti-Tung protesters.
As it has done regarding contentious issues in the past — see here and here — Mission Local asked virtually every San Francisco elected official to go on the record. Our question:
“Should Nancy Tung resign from the DCCC and/or step down as San Francisco Democratic Party chair? Why or why not?”
Respondents who simply answered “yes” or “no” were urged to explain their answers. Some did. Most did not.
Finally, when faced with questions about Flock in August, Democratic Party members were urged in writing on their internal group chat to spurn inquiries from the media. Mission Local is informed that nothing of this sort happened regarding our recent query. Rather, we are told that phone calls were made.

Yes, she should step down as chair. Voters gave Nancy her DCCC seat, and they can decide what happens to it. The committee made her chair; the chair is responsible for the institution she leads, and the institution failed.

In 2024, the party built a process that promised to protect survivors. In August, that process ended with no finding on what happened and failed to protect the person who came forward. Brad Chapin spoke before the committee himself and called the process “a sham.” The party said his complaint fell outside its rules and endorsed the same day, 12 to 10.

That same month, Nancy used her authority as chair to keep a resolution restricting Flock off the agenda. A month later, two members with ties to Flock voted on Flock cameras, and her response was that the party had no rule against it. The chair has discretion, and she clearly uses it. Having no rule doesn't mean having no responsibility.

I appreciate that Nancy has since apologized for the endorsement and acknowledged that the party's framework “broke down and did not protect survivors.” That apology came nearly six weeks after the endorsement and after the party's candidate had already left the race. I don't doubt that Nancy is sorry, but accountability is not a judgment about whether someone feels remorse. It is about whether leadership carries consequences when an institution fails, and I don't believe the person who led the party through this failure should be the one to lead it out.

Who replaces her matters. A new chair backed by the same slate may change little. But who decided this has to be the status quo? The status quo isn't working for us, and uncertainty about what comes next isn't a reason to preserve what has already failed.

This is bigger than Nancy. The party told survivors it would protect them. When that promise was tested, its process failed the person who came forward, and the committee endorsed anyway. That cannot become our precedent. The next person weighing whether to come forward is watching what our party does now.

Every survivor, of every gender, deserves to be taken seriously by our leaders and by the public. Speaking up must cost less than staying silent.
